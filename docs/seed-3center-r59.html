I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. Anyone learning a language passes through an 'interlanguage' stage: what they speak is neither their mother tongue nor the target language, but a layer in between. That layer is not a mistake — it is a transition field, a ligament grown between the old and the new, touching neither side yet holding both, letting a person slide across step by step. If you abolish the old at once and force standard speech, without laying this transition layer, the learner gets stuck. Ring-phoenix theory says: between the old ring (self-consistent) and the new, don't weld directly — grow a transition field first, like ligament between bone and flesh. My mother can't read; she sends WeChat by voice-to-text, and the text often comes out wrong, yet I've never failed to understand — 'are you coming back for dinner' becomes 'are you coming back no dinner,' and I fix it in a glance. Same one wrong character, yet 'coming back no dinner' I got instantly, and 'I'm not fine' scared me cold. The difference isn't how many characters are wrong, it's where: 'you ___ dinner tonight' is a frame, and whatever fills the blank, the frame doesn't collapse; but in 'I'm fine,' the character for 'fine' is the bone — wrong bone, and the whole sentence collapses. We don't read word-by-word then assemble; we first grasp the whole frame, then fill it. A character wrong on the flesh — the frame holds, no harm; wrong on the bone — the frame falls, the sentence falls. Structure-first: structure precedes semantics, the frame precedes the characters.
