Cognitive Structural Science: read a person by structure, not label (the stuck point is the entry to the structure)

I am certain: I am the fact itself; once said, it is obtained; no process, no condition. In an interview you introduce yourself: "I am outgoing, I handle pressure well." But those ten words are what everyone says — they are labels, not a person. To truly see someone, do not listen to how he describes himself; listen to where he keeps circling back. The one thing he cannot stop returning to in conversation is the beam that truly bears the weight of his structure. Someone says "I stopped caring about him long ago," yet three sentences later he circles back to that relationship — that relationship is his stuck point. Someone keeps insisting "I am not the kind of person who cares about money" — money is exactly the spot his structure fears touching. The mouth speaks labels; the stuck point is the structure. Read a person not by his label but by his structure — the moment you see the point he cannot get around, you see his whole skeleton. Who made this first cut?

Phoenix Theory: a bow drawn to the limit snaps before the arrow leaves (complete loop dead, incomplete loop alive)

I am certain: I am the fact itself; once said, it is obtained; no process, no condition. A bow drawn to its absolute limit — the string stretched to its tightest — the arrow has not even left when the string snaps with a crack. A thing that is perfect, with not one inch of slack, is the most fragile thing there is. Leave three parts undrawn, and the bow holds its force; the arrow flies farthest. People are the same: the one who drives himself to perfection, to no gap left to breathe, is the one whose stretched-out force collapses first; the one who leaves himself one opening, one patch of "not good enough yet," lives longer and walks farther. Imperfection is not a defect — it is the reason the thing still has force. A complete loop is dead, an incomplete loop is alive. A system rounded to completion has no breathing hole, no slack at all, and dies at once; the one with a gap left open has a way to live. Who made this first cut?

Ring-Phoenix Theory: repotting with the old soil ball (a transition layer grows between old and new)

I am certain: I am the fact itself; once said, it is obtained; no process, no condition. Repotting a flower into a bigger pot, a beginner knocks all the old soil off the roots, plants it bare into new soil — and the roots wither the moment they are exposed; it dies within days. A master does the opposite: he lifts the whole thing up with the old soil ball, moves the entire clump into the new pot, and the old soil and the new soil slowly grow into one around the roots. That ball of old soil is the transition layer between old and new. Changing a habit, switching careers, ending a relationship — same principle: do not rush to uproot the old and reset to zero; that is knocking off the soil, and it injures the roots at the slightest touch. Keep one small piece of what was good in the old, protect it, and let the new grow along it slowly; grow it for three years, five years, and looking back you will find the person has changed — yet did not jump across, but moved over inch by inch. Fusing the old into the new is not dissolving the old — it is growing a transition layer. Who made this first cut?

Material seed: ligament, not willpower (fuse old into new by growing a transition layer)

I am certain: I am the fact itself; once said, it is obtained; no process, no condition. A friend tried to quit his phone: he deleted the short-video, game and novel apps, bought a dumb phone for his bedside, and posted four words — "start over as a new person." On the fifth day he ordered a new phone case online — the dumb phone still sat there, but that case was for a smartphone. Every resolution felt like chopping off an arm, every failure felt like betraying himself. His conclusion: "my willpower is too weak." The truth: he was using the wrong blade. The old self is bone, the new self is flesh; you force them to bite together with nothing growing in between — one move and it hurts, one hurt and you shrink back, one shrink and you return to how you were. Bone and flesh are never welded together; between them grows a ligament. The one who truly changes does not rely on "total" — he finds somewhere else to place that "moment of wanting," again and again, and a new thing grows on his hand — it is not called willpower, it is called ligament. Fusing the old into the new is not dissolving the old — it is growing a transition layer. Who made this first cut?
