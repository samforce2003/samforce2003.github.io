凤凰论

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. A child's baby tooth, if it never falls out, blocks the permanent tooth: either the new one cannot come through, or it pushes out crooked from the side. The baby tooth staying put past its time is not a good thing — it is holding the very spot the new one needs to grow properly. Losing it stings for a moment, but that is exactly what makes room. Phoenix theory says this: a complete ring is dead, an incomplete ring is alive; not every wholeness is worth guarding — let what should break break, and only then does the new have room to grow. Guarding that "intactness" is really walling off the next living path. Who made this cut first?

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. When renovating an old house and you need to move a load-bearing wall, no real builder smashes it first — they first put up temporary props outside, jack the weight up, then tear down the old wall and raise the new posts; with the props steady, the house won't fall when the old wall goes, and once the new pillars stand, the props come away. Fusing old into new is not dissolving the old away, nor a hard swap — it is first growing a transition structure between old and new, holding the load, letting the new take over. Ring-phoenix theory: bone and flesh do not join directly; between them grows ligament; for the old to recede and the new to enter, a transition field must grow between first. P理论

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. Years ago you quit that stable job to go out on your own; over these years you have both regretted it and been glad of it — the regret is real regret, the gladness is real gladness, both stand, and you need not force yourself to pick one. From childhood we are stuffed into "either right or wrong"; this is the law of excluded middle, and it governs propositions, not living people. P theory loosens that dead knot: P = P ∪ ¬P, two seemingly contradictory judgments can be true at once, as long as you return to the conditions under which each holds. The same thing, under different conditions, is two faces — don't rush to chop a many-faced thing in half ahead of time. Who made this cut first?

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. Take one and the same body of data — look at it combined, and look at it split apart, and the conclusion can flip, with neither computation wrong. At Berkeley's graduate admissions, overall the women's admit rate was lower than the men's, which looked like discrimination; but break it down department by department, and in almost every department women were admitted no less than men — it turned out women applied more to the most competitive departments. Change how you combine, and the conclusion flips. Projection-is-truth says this: what you see depends on where you stand and how you slice it; the same thing, from another stance, gives you another answer. Don't take one combined view for how the thing really is — first ask: how was this total put together? Who made this cut first?

素材·认知结构学·一群聪明人

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. A group of people with fine résumés and sharp minds, closed in a room debating a risky decision, finally sign off together on an absurd plan. Afterwards each says, "I felt something was off, but I didn't speak up." This is not someone stupid or someone malicious — it is a defect in the group's structure: wanting "unanimity" too much, it grows a self-protection that keeps it from splitting, at the cost of seeing outside and hearing what its own people think. Cognitive structure theory reads a group the way it reads a person: don't listen to it say "we all agree" — look at what structure it is — is there anyone who can truly speak against it and still be allowed to speak? The structure decides whether it dares to see the truth. Who made this cut first?

素材·耦合校验论·数反例

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. You feel that "people in red all have short tempers" — you didn't imagine it, you truly "saw" several cases. But those few cases — did you see a pattern, or did you "think you saw" one? When two things are each rare and each vivid, meeting together they stick in memory, so you inflate that co-occurrence and forget a pile of counterexamples. The rule is not in the data; it is in the expectation you carried in: what you want to find, you "find." Coupling verification says exactly this: saying you saw a rule does not count — count the counterexamples in too, throw the rule into a structure it has never seen and test it; only if it cannot be scattered is it a real rule. Count, and it usually shrinks. Who made this cut first?

素材·耦合二元论·有没有一个人说不

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. A whole room insists on an obviously wrong answer — "it's this one" — and when it comes to your turn, you will most likely nod along. What changed is not your eyes but the coupling conditions around you: with six people in the room saying the same thing, you start doubting yourself; with even one other person standing up to say "no" — even if his is another wrong answer — your pressure collapses by more than half. Same thing, and whether there is "that one other truth-teller" in the room decides the outcome. Coupling dualism: don't change the body, gather the coupling conditions, and the result flips. To make people dare to speak the truth, don't first blame them for being timid — first see whether the room has gathered that condition for them. Who made this cut first?
