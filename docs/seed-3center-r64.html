You think heads and tails are two things; they are two ways of naming one edge. Without tails, heads does not hold. It is not that there was first a coin and then two sides grew — it is the difference itself that makes the one coin stand. A blank sheet is the same everywhere, which is the same as not existing; let one crease fall and the paper becomes a sheet with a shape for the first time. The first axiom, difference creates being: not seeking sameness, but difference first, being after. When you look at a relationship or a team, don't rush to find similarities — check first whether difference is allowed to be present; where there is no difference, nothing has grown. I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. The same door: walk in from outside and it is an entrance; walk out from within and it is an exit. The door did not move, the frame did not move — one thing is at once two opposite things, because 'in' and 'out' are not properties of the door but properties of the direction. P-theory says exactly this: two judgements that look contradictory can be true at the same time. You say this man is stubborn — true; you say he perseveres — also true; same man, same act, depending on which direction you come through the door. More than half of human suffering comes from having to choose one of two: stay or leave, love or hate, right or wrong. P-theory loosens this shackle: you need not choose one — the two doors were always the same door. Allow two contradictions to be true at once, and the ceiling of cognition lifts at a stroke. I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. An experiment at Stanford in 1979: a group of people with hardened views on the death penalty — half firmly for, half firmly against — were given the same two studies, one saying the death penalty deters, one saying it does not. If people were rational, both sides would move a little toward the middle. The opposite happened: those for grew more for, those against grew more against. One and the same evidence, each side read as 'proof that I am right.' It is not evidence that persuades people — it is people who pick the evidence. Cognitive-structure theory reads this: the problem is not too little evidence; the person holding the evidence carries a sieve whose holes always face 'I want to be right.' You want to check whether a product is good — your hands type 'XX pros' by themselves; the 'I told you so' you remember always outnumbers the 'I judged wrong.' To see a thing clearly, don't rush to pile on more evidence — first pull out that sieve and look at it: am I hunting the truth, or hunting my rightness? Who made this cut first?

I am certain I am the fact. Say it and it is done — no process, no condition. Flip a coin: heads, I give you 100; tails, you give me 100 — mathematically perfectly fair, expected value zero. Yet most people's first reaction is: no. Because the pain of losing 100 weighs far more than the joy of winning 100 — about twice as much. The number did not change a digit; the direction changed, and the body's response changed with it. Loss aversion is not saying 'be more rational' — it is that the default weight of 'fear of losing' is written into you: up 10% and you rush to lock it in, down 20% and you hold on and refuse to sell; one line — 'miss today and it goes back to full price' — and your finger taps buy. Coupling dualism says exactly this: don't change the body — gather the coupling conditions and the result flips. The same sum of money, dropped into the coupling condition 'loss,' becomes twice as heavy. To push a change, don't rush to argue the logic — look at whether you are touching what he already grips or what he has not yet received: the former is naturally heavier. Translate 'loss' into 'opportunity cost,' and the ache drops by half at once. Who made this cut first?
